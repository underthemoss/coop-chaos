# The trees remember

Trees are members of the kingdom. A chicken may live for a tree, quarrel with a tree, leave a tree, and eventually bring her children back to it. The forest is a society whose oldest citizens cannot simply get up and follow everybody else.

**Status:** the user has accepted named trees with personalities, species-based families, changeable chicken affiliations, and capabilities made possible by a kingdom containing several tree families. The specific names, family treatments, relationships, and examples below are proposed creative canon. These systems are not implemented. The existing construction slice has named home sites and earned chicken attachment; that is a starting point, not evidence of a tree social simulation.

## Personhood at the speed of a tree

Trees have desires and memories, but their attention moves differently from a chicken's. A chicken remembers who took her perch yesterday. Morrow remembers the first winter anybody trusted his branches. A hen wants a ladder by dusk. Her tree wonders whether the ladder will still be loved when the hen's grandchildren are old.

Their agency appears in shelter, posture, seasonal growth, the places they are willing to host, and what they remember. A tree can be generous, vain, watchful, impatient with clutter, or reluctant to be needed again. These are individual temperaments. An oak need not be wise; a young birch may be a dreadful traditionalist.

The proposed tone is quiet, slow, and occasionally funny. No human faces in bark, walking tree people, or constant voiced conversations. Selected story moments may translate a tree's impression into a short line: “Morrow has noticed that supper happens somewhere else now.” Physical reactions stay within the agreed fiction and timescale. Instant branch gestures, overnight mature growth, or flowers functioning as a universal mood meter would weaken the trees' presence.

This is deliberate fantasy personification. The botanical references below support forms and habitats, not claims that real trees have human memories, consent, or political opinions.

## Five things that must stay distinct

| Identity | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Botanical family | A fictional cultural grouping rooted in a tree species or related kind | Oak kin |
| Seed ancestry | Actual descent, where known | A sapling grown from Morrow's acorn |
| Growth form | The physical arrangement of useful branches and spaces | Longbough, Crowncourt, Windward |
| Individual | A particular named tree with a temperament and history | Morrow |
| Kingdom | A political community of trees and chickens | A kingdom containing Morrow and two unrelated willows |

“Family” is an in-world kinship word, not the formal botanical taxonomic rank. A Longbough is not a species. Similar growth forms can occur in several families, subject to believable anatomy. Two oaks need not be relatives or political allies; an oak and a willow can consider themselves chosen family.

## Four founding tree families

These are a first creative palette, not an exhaustive biome list or mandatory four-part technology key. Each family needs individual variation in age, temperament, silhouette, useful spaces, and history.

### Oak kin — the rooms that endure

Broad forks, muscular limbs, irregular crowns, and a generous root court make an oak feel like a place people have already started gathering. Open-grown white oak is one useful reference for a broad, spreading crown; it is not a specification for every oak. [University of Nebraska tree reference](https://snr.unl.edu/data/trees/identification/basictree.aspx?sid=15).

**Morrow** is the first proposed individual: a broad old tree with one long, slightly descending arm and a scar where lightning once took the other. He likes ordinary noise. He believes a good home should always have room for one more bird, then quietly resents the new bridge that makes his court a thoroughfare. His generosity and possessiveness can coexist.

Oak-associated traditions might include an empty place kept at supper for somebody away and the preservation of the first crude roost beneath later grand houses. Neither tradition belongs automatically to every oak or every bird raised there.

Spatial promise: substantial shared decks and courts where several routes meet. Cost: a crowded central junction can become a bottleneck; deep shade and sprawling buildings compete with readable ground work. Attachment to an old center can make a necessary new district politically awkward.

### Willow kin — the rooms that bend

A willow's long hanging twigs can make veils, sheltered edges, and moving curtains of light. They should frame the view rather than erase the chickens behind foliage. Weeping willow provides an explicit caution: flexible drooping twigs do not imply strong structural timber. [Utah State University tree reference](https://extension.usu.edu/treebrowser/catalog/willow-weeping).

**Saffron** grows beside a shallow bank, bent away from the wind. She remembers the names of birds who stop to rest. She welcomes visitors but hates being treated as a shortcut: hauling traffic wears down the quiet bank where her first guests once slept. She can be hospitable without agreeing to become a public road.

Willow-associated craft develops around carefully gathered flexible material and woven shade or screens. Large homes still require credible supported anchors; a dangling twig cannot carry a district. Water access and soft ground create tradeoffs, not a free all-purpose resource bonus.

Spatial promise: low sheltered retreats, weaving courts, screened crossings when supported by suitable anchors. Cost: weak limbs, delicate banks, and clear limits on heavy occupation. Personality is not a permission to ignore structure.

### Pine kin — the rooms that look outward

Use crooked trunks, visible branch tiers, and separated needle tufts. Mature Japanese white pine offers a useful alternative to the rejected stack-of-cones silhouette: a spreading, irregular tree with exposed structure. [Penn State Extension reference](https://extension.psu.edu/japanese-white-pine-whimsical-and-tough).

**Needlewick** likes being first to notice a storm and second to admit that he was frightened. He takes lookout duty personally. A new home that blocks his outward view bothers him more than a scratched trunk. He proudly shelters a bird who cannot fly, once the kingdom gives her an honest low berth and a usable route upward.

Pine-associated traditions might celebrate the first watch of a youngster, with an older bird quietly keeping a second watch below. They need not create a military caste or aggressive tree family.

Spatial promise: separated lookout places and distinctive vertical routes. Cost: exposed approaches, longer hauling, wind, and access requirements. Height helps a watcher; it is not universal predator immunity.

### Birch kin — the rooms that let light through

Pale slender stems and a light crown break up heavier groves. Paper birch offers references for ascending branches and a sometimes-curved trunk. Its peeling bark is a visual cue, not permission for endless harvesting from living trees. [Utah State University tree reference](https://extension.usu.edu/treebrowser/catalog/birch-paper).

**Pale Nell** stands at the edge of a cluster and is intensely interested in whoever passes. She likes new ideas, then worries that nobody will stay long enough to finish one. She asks for a small home that is used every day rather than the grand lookout somebody has promised her three times.

Birch-associated districts may favor small light rooms and open gathering places. A slim tree is not a scaled-down oak: load and available branch space constrain its architecture. Individual age, condition, and growth matter more than a blanket species statistic.

Spatial promise: light courts, smaller homes, and legible transitions between forest and commons. Cost: limited heavy support and exposure at the edge. A birch can become a cherished home without becoming the biggest building platform.

## Belonging is earned, and can change

A bird's lineage does not assign its kingdom, favorite tree, job, morality, or spouse. Upbringing establishes a history, not destiny. A Mossback raised beside Saffron may love her woven shadows. A Cloudplume may adore Needlewick's outlook but need a low natural fork until access is built. A tiny Thimblewing may prefer Morrow's noisy court to the highest branch available.

Keep two directions of relationship:

- **Chicken → tree:** familiarity, comfort, gratitude, remembered safety, pride in work, grievance, and preferred places.
- **Tree → household or coop:** history of care, promises kept, tolerated burdens, valued company, and unresolved injury.

These are contextual memories and needs, not two universal approval meters. A bird can love a tree and distrust the household controlling its ladder. A tree can trust a family with a nursery and reject a proposed crowded market court. Political membership and access agreements are separate from affection.

Migration should leave evidence. A departed hen's old perch remains in the chronicle; her former tree may remember her, and returning can reopen a relationship. Do not erase a life history when a preferred-home field changes. Visitors, fostered chicks, marriages, friendships, and joint work can all connect families without converting individuals into trade inventory.

## More families, more ways to build

The benefit of a mixed grove is complementarity: different physical places, locally available materials, remembered crafts, and relationships. The dependency is **a useful combination that chickens actually organize and build**. Counting species and awarding a percentage bonus would miss the idea.

| Proposed capability | What actually enables it | Consequence and alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A sheltered crossing between districts | Credible structural anchors, flexible woven screening, learned craft, hauled material, and a built route | Oak and willow neighbors can make this locally convenient. Another kingdom can trade for weaving material or use another suitable design. |
| A counterweight delivery hoist | A strong landing and support, suitable components, hauling knowledge, counterweight, labor, and maintained access | A broad home tree and a nearby lookout district create a reason to invent it. No pine-presence flag magically unlocks machinery. |
| A quiet nursery near a busy court | An accessible sheltered location, low-traffic route, actual food delivery, and household/tree agreement | Several tree forms offer more placement choices. A single-family grove can reserve and improve a suitable site. |
| Storm refuge with another family | Spare usable berths, a safe route, supplies, and a relationship that permits refuge | Diversity may provide different exposures and layouts; the actual storm and sites determine safety. No universal mixed-forest immunity. |
| A later powered lift district | Research and demonstrated work, stable structure, power, components, safe landings, maintenance | Advanced technology follows needs and inventions. Four botanical families are never a compulsory collectible key. |

These are fictional design recipes and dependencies, not engineering guidance. Do not promise that a particular real species is sufficient for a safe structure.

A single-family kingdom must remain viable, distinctive, and fun. Mixed families expand local options and sometimes make a chain easier; trade, visiting craftspeople, adapted construction, and knowledge sharing provide other paths. Diversity is desirable because it changes possibilities and relationships, not because the game punishes a player who likes an oak kingdom.

## Care, agreement, and productive friction

Living host trees are not disposable lumber nodes. Fallen material, agreed pruning, and deliberate cultivation can supply construction, with finite availability and visible recovery. Do not implement limitless bark scraping, instant regrowth, or the destruction of a home tree as an unnoticed resource click.

Routine building within an established agreement can proceed normally. Reserve negotiation for an intrusive new use, a meaningful burden, or a broken promise. A tree should not demand dialogue for every ladder rung. A disagreement cannot instantly delete occupied homes or strand birds by removing their route; changes need warning, an intelligible consequence, and a chance to adapt.

Separate structural condition, capacity, exposure, and access from personality. A cheerful tree cannot support an impossible load. A sulking tree still physically shelters the birds already asleep in its branches. Care may change future agreement and long-term health; it should not be a disguised happiness tax.

Trees age on a scale that gives memory weight. A newly planted seed is a promise to the future, not a mature capital before the first thirty-minute session ends. Any compressed growth must be explicit world fiction and an intentional progression decision.

## Stories the construction system should be able to produce

**The bridge that moved supper.** Chickens build a useful crossing from Morrow toward Saffron. Deliveries become shorter; several birds begin lingering at the new woven landing. Morrow is pleased by their work but misses the evening gathering. The player can preserve an old meeting place, establish a shared routine, or let the community move. The outcome changes remembered use and belonging, not a hidden productivity punishment.

**The wrong kind of bird for the right tree.** Mallow, a Cloudplume, loves Needlewick. Her body cannot make the flight required by the high homes. She has a valid low berth while the coop builds access. Finishing the ladder creates a new usable destination and a remembered act of inclusion. Her desire is neither overwritten by lineage nor granted by a fake flight animation.

**The first guest after the storm.** A damaged route forces an oak household to stay with Saffron. A previously aloof hen carries supplies for the host. Shared work and refuge start a cross-family friendship. Repairing the original route gives the household a real choice about where to return.

**The sapling nobody has to love.** An acorn from Morrow survives at the edge of the commons. Its ancestry is known, but its future politics are not. Chickens can care for it, a rival can shelter it, and it may grow up with customs unlike Morrow's. Seed ancestry is continuity, not ownership.

**The magnificent unused room.** Pale Nell receives an elaborate platform nobody visits because the route is poor. The solution may be a path and an everyday task, not another decoration. A building matters because somebody can reach it, use it, and remember it.

## First future implementation proof

Do not build all four families or a sprawling relationship engine at once. The useful next narrative proof is two named trees with different spaces, one bird whose past and current preference differ, and one completed construction chain that changes access and produces a persistent, visible relationship consequence. Save and reload the identities, memory, physical access, and consequence together. Include a single-family alternative so complementarity is demonstrated without a diversity gate.

Keep that future proof separate from the current art proof: one production tree and a few chicken bodies at the actual gameplay camera, with delivery, occupancy, night, and overlapping crowns.
