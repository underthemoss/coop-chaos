# Life above the ground

## Direction and scope

The accepted direction begins with chickens living and working on the ground,
then flying into trees to roost at night. They earn their way toward elaborate
treehouses, ladders, and eventually science-fiction elevators and defenses.
Their inventions should solve chicken problems and generate new social choices.

The progression and examples below develop that direction. Names, recipes,
research requirements, and numerical effects are design proposals. The current
implementation includes natural roosting, treehouses with ladders, an extra
sleeping deck, and a first hauling-basket technique learned by raising a real
construction bundle. Baskets subsequently lift deck deliveries; they do not yet
transport birds. Powered lifts, bridges and defenses below remain proposals.
[Settlement design](settlement-design.md) owns the wider game loop, and
[biology research](../docs/research/chicken-biology.md) distinguishes animal
constraints from this fictional civilization.

## A settlement in three layers

**The ground is busy.** Chickens scratch feeding beds, gather reeds, sort seed,
weave panels, and carry bundles between local caches. Dust grounds and feeding
junctions become social centers. Early settlements should look improvised:
carefully arranged resources, worn routes, and a few favored trees.

**The trunks become infrastructure.** Landings, ladders, braces, hauling rigs,
and passing perches connect ground work to life overhead. A wide trunk may
support several approaches; a single narrow landing can become a queue and a
source of influence. Goods must cross this space rather than vanish into a tree's
inventory. Nearby emergency branches can interrupt a dangerous journey.

**The canopy becomes a neighborhood.** Woven sleeping decks, sheltered brood
rooms, lookout branches, and connecting walks form recognizable districts.
Residences should retain the irregular shape of their trees. A broad low fork
and a narrow high crown invite different layouts; neither is always superior.

Natural roosting remains useful throughout progression. An adult who can fly to
a branch should not suddenly need an elevator after one is invented. Built
access becomes valuable for particular loads, bodies, conditions, and journeys.
Exact mobility limits belong to the simulation and must be visible to the player.

## Forest kingdoms and the open commons

Forest clusters are the natural centers of kingdoms in this fictional world.
A kingdom grows around occupied roosts, shared treehouses, dependable access,
and birds who recognize that place as home. Its shape follows connected trees
and the routes its community can maintain. A crown, flag, or colored map region
alone does not make an empty grove into a functioning settlement.

Open fields are no-man's-land: productive ground without dependable exclusive
control. Seed patches, insect-rich feeding grounds, water approaches, and building
materials draw birds out of their homes and bring different coops into contact.
This is a designed geography, not a claim that insects or food exist only in
grassland. Woodland litter and edges can also support foraging; the game should
make particular patches valuable rather than treating grass as complete nutrition.

The recurring journey is **leave the trees, gather in the commons, carry useful
goods home, and reach a roost before night**. The return matters as much as the
outbound trip. A distant rich patch can be less useful than a modest nearby one
when carrying distance, congestion, daylight, and protection are considered.
Food, territorial encounters, and predator pressures are later systems; the first
construction proof establishes the spatial journey with actual material carriers.

Three settlement positions should invite different choices:

| Position | Attraction | Pressure to resolve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inner grove | Room for a connected canopy neighborhood and several routes between homes. | Longer trips to open-land resources; traffic collects at woodland exits. |
| Forest edge | Short returns from feeding grounds and convenient ground-to-tree loading. | More exposed approaches; returning carriers and guards need usable landing space. |
| Separated copse | Access to a new gathering area and the seed of a second neighborhood. | An open crossing separates supplies, warning, assistance, and belonging. |

These are prospective tradeoffs, not automatic safety modifiers. Dense trees can
also conceal an approach; actual threats must follow their implemented behavior.
The first map should visibly establish a home grove, an edge, and open supply
ground without pretending the full risk model already exists.

Access technology changes this geography. A ladder improves one tree's connection
to the ground. A hauling basket makes its loading court important. A canopy bridge
joins nearby homes into a district, while a later lift can turn an overlooked
tree into a busy gateway. A bridge should respect span and supports; it cannot
erase every meadow with one cheap line. Upkeep and fallback routes keep the old
ground paths useful.

Later diplomacy can grow from repeated use of the commons: a shared dawn feeding
window, a promise to relay alarms, a disputed water approach, or a temporary escort
for a brood's crossing. A rooster who boasts of guarding a meadow must actually
show up; hens who keep a distant route supplied can acquire influence from that
work. Agreements provide limited, visible access and obligations, not instantaneous
ownership of every field. Specific territorial and pact rules remain proposals.

The grove should acquire a recognizable culture through its history. The first
treehouse might become the Old Nest even after grander homes surround it. A busy
edge landing can become the place returning foragers exchange news; a quieter
inner fork becomes a nursery neighborhood. Birds who routinely work the commons
may favor accommodation with outsiders, while a household that lost access to a
feeding patch presses for escorts. Those are possible responses to remembered
events, not fixed personalities assigned to a district or lineage.

Preserve the comic scale of these ambitions. A kingdom's ceremonial gate may be
two impressive twigs beside an extremely busy ladder. An argument about royal
precedence can block a basket of reeds. The player can solve the actual bottleneck
with a second landing, but the chickens may still remember who yielded first.

## Inventions and the problems they solve

| Stage | Invention | New possibility | New cost or tension |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First settlement | Ground caches and chosen natural roost trees | Organize work while retaining free flight to ordinary sleeping branches. | Evening travel, limited preferred perches, exposed supplies. |
| First shared home | Woven branch deck and rain hood | Create additional sheltered sleeping places; contributions become attached to a place. | Materials and work; competing claims to the nicest berths. |
| Trunk access | Gripping ladders, resting landings, passing perches | Make deliberate climbing routes and improve access for particular birds or loads. | Traffic capacity, upkeep, occupation of useful trunk space. |
| Bulk hauling | Counterweight baskets and beak-operated release rigs | Raise construction bundles without sending each bird up carrying one. | Operators, loading time, balance, and queues at the landing. |
| Canopy links | Suspended walks and covered branch bridges | Connect neighboring tree districts with predictable cargo routes. | Longer dependencies, maintenance, limited passing room, concentrated traffic. |
| Powered settlement | Sunscale collectors, stored power, powered hoists | Move larger loads and support night services independently of constant hauling labor. | Weather-dependent supply, reserves, cleaning, repair, and allocation priorities. |
| Science-fiction canopy | Featherlifts, automated cargo pods, responsive shelter | Build dense vertical neighborhoods with controlled movement and rapid protective responses. | Power and control dependencies, specialist maintenance, unequal access to service. |

These are branches of advancement, not a compulsory ladder of upgrades. A
compact coop may prefer redundant manual routes. A sprawling canopy district
may justify powered lifts. An old ladder remains useful as a fallback even when
a spectacular elevator occupies the same tree.

The first treehouse should already suggest this future: a woven floor following
a fork, a crooked but carefully braced access route, a roof curved around living
branches, and distinct sleeping places. Early construction should look ingenious
at chicken scale, with oversized knots, peg-sized perches, and room to turn with
a bundle. Later machinery can preserve those shapes in polished or luminous
materials. Avoid simply shrinking a human suburban house or skyscraper.

## Technology arrives through useful experiments

Prototype unlocks should come from an observed need, a supplied experiment, and
a working demonstration. For example, repeated congestion at a treehouse landing
reveals the value of bulk hauling. The player funds a test rig, assigns labor,
and supplies components. Its first successful delivery establishes a usable
technique. Ordinary buildings should not require repetitive research ceremonies.

Do not award a new machine solely because an invisible technology meter fills.
Show what was learned and what the invention enables. New knowledge can spread
through demonstration, migration, fostering, trade, or rivalry in later systems.
A skilled builder may seek recognition or better conditions; a technique should
not disappear from every chicken's mind when its inventor dies.

For the advanced canopy, **Sunscale** is a provisional name for lightweight
energy collectors arranged like leaves. **Featherlift** names a powered vertical
lift with grasping perches and separate cargo space. Their energy sources and
working rules are fictional technology to design explicitly. Real reproductive
eggs never become batteries, fuel, or research currency.

## Defenses answer particular threats

Defense progression should improve detection, access control, exposure, escape,
or recovery. It should not reduce the entire landscape to a universal armor
number. Predator behavior must interact with the same paths and places used by
the economy.

| Proposed defense | Intended job | Remaining pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Watch branches and practiced alarm responses | Notice an approach and give nearby workers time to respond. | Watchers need visibility, attention, rest, and a way for the warning to travel. |
| Woven overhead screens and covered runs | Interrupt exposed approaches and connect useful refuge. | Ground access and gaps between structures still matter. |
| Thorn aprons and guarded trunk approaches | Make selected ground routes harder to traverse. | Defenders still need sight lines; construction can obstruct their own traffic. |
| Several escape landings | Offer alternatives when one approach is blocked. | Landings need enough space and routes birds can actually use. |
| Relay perches and later powered warning networks | Spread a detected threat across several districts. | A warning must originate somewhere; an isolated outpost is not omniscient. |
| Responsive canopy shutters and protected lifts | Secure occupied places or evacuate birds during a known threat. | Power, maintenance, evacuation capacity, and failure states matter. |

These are proposed game functions. Species-specific effects require their own
research and encounter tests. A raised home does not automatically solve every
predator. A closed protective shutter may also interrupt a supply route; its
operator must know whether a carrier is still outside. A power shortage can
force a choice between throughput and protection, with time to respond before
catastrophe rather than a surprise punishment.

## Small habits create a history

Give progress physical memories. A hen who helped weave the first sheltered
deck starts sleeping there. A proud carrier repeatedly arrives by flight even
after a ladder is built. A wounded bird begins using the lift and depends on its
operator. An older rooster claims the sunrise perch; younger birds gradually
establish another gathering point.

These examples become story only when behavior follows actual capabilities,
preferences, experience, and access. The player should be able to change the
conditions. Build another landing and a dispute may fade. Concentrate every
delivery at one feeding platform and the same birds may acquire disproportionate
influence. A memorial perch can preserve a lost protector's history while
remaining a real place used by living chickens.

Comedy comes from dignity and effort: an elaborate pulley for one stubborn
bundle, a proud unveiling delayed by a queue of impatient hens, a magnificent
elevator beside a rooster who insists on flying. Those moments should arise
while the settlement works, without repeatedly stopping play for dialogue.

## Boundaries for the first playable chain

Implement ground work, natural night flight, delivered construction materials,
and an earned treehouse improvement with usable sheltered perches. Make its
benefit observable through occupancy, rest, and a small persistent attachment.
Treat its ladder as a real access feature within the implemented mobility rules.
Keep stores, cargo, work, flight, and sleeping positions coherent across saves.

Do not fill the interface with disabled controls for all the technologies above.
The first settlement should be complete enough to operate on its own. Its shapes,
locations, and useful construction should make the next possibility easy to imagine.
