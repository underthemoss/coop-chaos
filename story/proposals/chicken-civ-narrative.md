# Chicken Civ: narrative and campaign design

24 October 2026 · Narrative draft 1

**Status:** warfare and territorial conquest are the user-selected main goal.
The particular history, cast, institutions, dialogue, campaign and response rules
below are proposed creative canon. They do not replace the current playable
fixture or become true of an existing save. [World rules](../world.md) and
[tree kinships](../tree-kinships.md) retain personhood and affiliation boundaries.
[Gameplay design](chicken-civ-design.md) defines the proposed mechanical causes.

## 1. The emotional promise

Build a kingdom with people you recognize. Win ground that changes their lives.
Discover that the way you won it has become part of the kingdom.

The pleasure is commanding an ingenious, ambitious civilization: commissioning
a working grove, organizing its forces, outmaneuvering a rival and watching a
new holding become useful. The emotional stakes come from recognizing who made
that success possible and who paid for it. Comedy respects the seriousness with
which chickens regard their own plans. War can be proud, frightening, cruel,
necessary, opportunistic and occasionally ridiculous.

The player should want to win. The story does not chastise every military order
or replace conquest with an inevitable lesson that fighting was pointless.
It gives particular wars particular motives and consequences. A generous ruler
can pursue expansion; an excellent protector can become a tyrant; a rival can
be brave and still be an obstacle the player must defeat.

## 2. Why kingdoms fight

The proposed setting begins with inhabited tree groves separated by productive
commons. For generations, communities used some of those places through local
custom: a morning feeding window, a shared crossing, a promise to shelter visitors,
or a turn at a productive insect bed. Such arrangements worked while demand,
travel and military reach remained limited.

Improved hauling, storage and fortification allow a coop to maintain an armed
presence farther from home. An old promise of access can now be enforced, narrowed
or broken by someone who can keep a garrison there. A productive grove can sustain
another household and a forward force. Communities begin turning use into claims,
and claims into kingdoms.

This is a proposed source of conflict, not a universal resource apocalypse.
There is enough room for ambition and disagreement. One coop seeks a dependable
protein source for a growing generation; another wants control of a crossing
that made it prosperous; a commander wants a victory to secure her position;
a tree fears being abandoned when supply shifts elsewhere. The same place can
matter to several parties for different reasons.

No humans built this world. Trees are named participants with long histories.
Chickens construct with beaks and feet, use natural branches, and develop their
own technology. Forest families are cultural possibilities rather than national
or moral castes. Every proposed coop can contain several tree families and many
individual dispositions.

## 3. The player's authority

For the first campaign, propose that the player guides a continuing coop council.
This provides clear authority to allocate supplies, commission buildings, muster
forces and choose campaigns without making one rooster the only person whose
will matters. The player is not a selectable ruler whose accidental death ends
the game. The [Long Crow progression proposal](chicken-civ-progression.md) develops
provisional offices, the Empty Perch succession custom, and Tansy's career.
Those examples remain open to creative refinement.

Orders have practical force but do not erase personhood. An adult's current
commitments and known limits appear before assignment. Most ordinary accepted
work proceeds without dialogue. Resistance becomes a decision when it is material:
a caregiver cannot abandon an active dependent without replacement; a veteran
may refuse a commander who deserted her; a resident may defend a captured home
without accepting the occupier as legitimate.

The political cost of an order should be local and evidenced. Do not charge a
generic tyranny currency whenever the player mobilizes a squad. If a commander
promised relief and keeps the same exhausted guard in place, record that broken
promise and its effect on that guard and witnesses. The player can repair the
condition, negotiate an exception, replace the person or accept a worsening tie.
The first mechanical proof need not implement every response listed here.

## 4. Communities with incompatible interests

These are provisional campaign identities, not different biological races.
They share the same fundamental economy and can learn the same basic military
roles. Distinction comes from place, institutions, available knowledge and history.

| Community | What it wants | What supports it | Internal disagreement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rootward | Secure enough nearby productive ground to expand without dependence on a rival's permission | Short internal routes and a community practiced at shared work | Carriers want dependable access; an ambitious commander wants a larger victory |
| Highcanopy | Keep the approaches that sustain its established influence | Prepared positions and experience supplying separated homes | Some residents favor negotiated access; others fear that yielding once will dissolve every claim |
| Reedwake's residents, in a later scenario | Retain a viable home and meaningful say over who uses it | Knowledge of the ground, supplies and the tree's history | Some want protection, some autonomy, and some advantage from the contest |

The first small gameplay test can use Rootward and Highcanopy with an uninhabited
central occupation site. Reedwake's civilian community belongs to a later narrative
scenario; it must not be claimed as implemented occupation politics.

## 5. Proposed people and trees

The following cast is an example campaign ensemble. The current Fern, Rook,
Dapple and Wren fixture is not renamed or made the mandatory opening cast.
Characters should be introduced through visible work before long biographies.

| Person | Want and fear | Useful contribution | Relationship pressure and possible change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tansy, a hen responsible for stores | Wants every expedition to have a way home; fears promises made against food that does not exist | Knows local stocks and repeatedly provisions difficult journeys | Respects a commander who returns unused supplies; challenges one who blames carriers for an underprovisioned campaign |
| Bracken, a rooster and field captain | Wants to become the protector the coop relies on; fears being remembered as timid | Holds a retreat long enough for others to escape | Pride can turn a sound defense into an unnecessary stand; relief or rescue can soften a rivalry |
| Sedge, a hen and mobile fighter | Wants credit for finding the winning route; fears being used as expendable reconnaissance | Scouts alternative approaches and raids exposed supply | May support ambitious campaigns but resent a captain who takes all the credit |
| Moss, a hen and experienced caregiver | Wants the next generation and wounded adults to receive dependable care; fears a succession of campaigns with no recovery | Makes the human cost of mobilization visible through actual care work | Can support a necessary campaign while demanding that a named promise of relief be kept |
| Vetch, a hen commanding the rival | Wants a defensible frontier and political standing at home; fears concessions will invite encirclement | Organizes reserves and counterattacks the supply supporting a new holding | Can respect a competent opponent and still contest every strategically useful grove |
| Flint, a rooster and rival scout | Wants to return with useful information and retain the trust of his companions | Reveals weakly held approaches and carries knowledge between groups | A witnessed rescue or abandoned companion can change his view of both commanders |

**Reedwake**, a proposed willow individual, values the repeated coming and going
that has kept its workcourt alive. It fears becoming a deserted trophy or an
overburdened fortress. Its willow family, its personal ancestry, and its particular
branch form are separate facts. It may tolerate an occupying garrison while
remaining attached to households that fled. Detailed expressions of tree agency
remain within the slow, restrained approach of tree kinships.

Do not turn these personalities into inevitable scripts. Tansy does not always
oppose war. Bracken is not guaranteed to sacrifice himself. Vetch does not switch
allegiance after one kind act. Their later behavior follows the actual record.

## 6. A proposed campaign arc

The cast must include consequential hen and rooster narratives throughout these
campaigns. The [hen and rooster narrative commitments](#hen-and-rooster-narrative-commitments)
below make their personal arcs and coverage explicit.

Use a sequence of bounded conflicts to introduce deeper decisions. Each scenario
can begin with an explicit established settlement so it does not require a long
mandatory domestic opening. Objectives are military; economic and personal
consequences persist where a campaign implementation explicitly supports them.

| Scenario | Military goal | Economic or exploration question | Narrative consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Shared Ground | Take and sustain the central grove against a rival | How many adults can leave work, and which approach did scouting reveal? | The first victory gives specific people credit, wounds and claims on what comes next |
| Beyond the Return | Secure a second holding with a separated supply route | Can a forward store and reserves support a longer campaign, or should the player strike a nearer target? | A broken relief promise or a successful withdrawal reshapes trust in command |
| The Occupied Home | Defeat a defended grove and keep it functioning | What can be seized, what must be supplied, and which residents will cooperate? | Military control and allegiance visibly diverge; residents retain earlier ties |
| A Kingdom to Keep | Hold several objectives through a coordinated counterattack | Can the player consolidate without stripping another district, and where will the rival actually strike? | The resulting kingdom reflects the households preserved, obligations kept and enemies made |

These are proposed scenario briefs, not an approved campaign production scope.
The first strategic prototype needs only the first contest. Later scenarios
require their stated systems, especially inhabited occupation and persistent
campaign continuity, rather than dialogue pretending those systems already work.

Victories can be tactical, costly, decisive or narrow. Defeat should show how
the opponent converted its opportunities: a missed scout report, an empty field
store, a force committed too far from home. Where the scenario allows retreat,
the survivors can become the basis of a later recovery. Do not silently rescue
the player with invented forces or require continued campaign support before it
has been designed.

## 7. How events become narrative

### Hen and rooster narrative commitments

Both hens and roosters must have desires beyond their current jobs, relationships
that matter to them, and consequential choices in war and ordinary life. Neither
is background scenery for the other's story. Sex can matter through bodies,
reproduction, courtship and particular community expectations, but no universal
rule assigns authority, courage, care, engineering or ambition by sex. Specific
customs are fictional institutions that people can support, dispute or change.

**Bracken, rooster: the protector who must learn to return.** His pride rests on
being the last to retreat. At first that makes him a convincing captain; later
it can keep exhausted companions at a position whose military value has gone.
His private wish is to finish a sheltered home and have evenings in which nobody
needs his courage. He has promised to relieve a named caregiver after the campaign.
The actual appointment and household ties must be established in the scenario,
not inferred from his sex or invented after the outcome.

His decisive moment is choosing whether to keep a public boast or organize a
withdrawal that brings his people home. A successful withdrawal can earn respect
from some companions and scorn from another; neither response is universal.
After injury, he can learn logistics or take a care shift while another adult
commands. His story continues through what he does with reduced mobility and
changed standing. Death is a possible consequence, not the required completion
of a rooster's arc. Survival, family life and relinquishing command can matter.

**Flint, rooster: the rival scout with two homes in memory.** He wants his reports
to be believed and his companions to return. A proposed later campaign gives him
an earlier attachment to a grove now held by the player. That attachment does not
make him a secret ally: people he loves can live under a government he opposes.
He may warn Vetch about a vulnerable supply landing while also seeking a permitted
way to help an old household evacuate. These actions need an actual relationship,
access and observed opportunity, not omniscient knowledge or a scripted betrayal.
His arc can end in continued rivalry, negotiated service, migration or an earned
change of allegiance. Romance is optional; recognition and belonging suffice.

**Tansy and Vetch, hens: authority with a life beyond competence.** Tansy's career
from carrier to quartermaster is detailed in the [progression proposal](chicken-civ-progression.md).
Give her a personal stake in a reachable home and in having a successor so she
can sometimes leave the depot. Vetch's wish to defend her frontier can conflict
with residents who want shared access. Her courage does not make her politically
right, and her ambition need not make her cruel. Neither woman's authority is a
temporary exception awaiting the arrival of a rooster.

**Moss and Sedge, hens: different ambitions within the same society.** Moss values
care and wants relief, recognition and a say in campaigns whose costs arrive at
her shelter. She can care deeply while resenting the assumption that she will
always remain behind. Sedge wants discovery, credit and a chance to command a
difficult expedition; she may also choose a household commitment that changes
when she can serve. A hen who lays or undertakes active care needs actual support
and a meaningful stance. Eggs, partners and household moves never become rewards
assigned to her by the player's promotion menu. Neither hen needs motherhood or
marriage to acquire a complete story.

For each authored campaign, review the cast for both hen and rooster agency:
each sex should have a consequential choice, an ordinary-life relationship and
a persistent response. Check the actual event and available action, not only the
pronouns or head count. Across the wider cast, include leadership and domestic
life for both. A small scenario need not force every theme into every character,
and no event should fire merely to satisfy a representation checklist.

### Mechanical events and persistent responses

Narrative follows an observed incident with participants, place, cause and result.
Store the facts needed to explain a response: who issued or accepted a commitment,
who performed the work, who was present, what changed and which prior relationship
made it meaningful. Internal motives can remain uncertain; physical events cannot.

| Mechanical event | Individual interpretation | Proposed persistent response | Player remedy or consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A squad returns after its promised relief time because supply was diverted | A guard believes another household was favored | Reduced willingness to accept that same open-ended duty | Set a shorter duty, supply relief, acknowledge the choice or knowingly accept resistance |
| A carrier is evacuated by a former rival | The rescue challenges an existing grievance | Willingness to travel together improves in that context | A new escort pairing becomes practical; the old conflict need not disappear |
| A scout's report leads to a successful flank but someone else receives credit | The scout resents the public account | Seeks recognition or challenges the next assignment | Credit the actual contribution or manage the resulting tension |
| A captured post is stocked while nearby civilian homes are neglected | Residents see occupation serving only the garrison | Less voluntary work or local cooperation | Supply the homes, negotiate a contribution or maintain a more costly military presence |
| A commander abandons an injured companion while retreating | Survivors disagree about necessity and betrayal | Specific trust changes among witnesses | Later choices can justify, repair or deepen the division; no universal moral verdict |
| A new home is kept supplied despite a counterattack | Residents connect safety to actual contributors | Stronger attachment and a remembered obligation | The place becomes part of a relationship, not just another controlled node |

Responses need a bounded rule and visible effect. Avoid a single global loyalty
score that makes affection, fear, duty, ambition and legitimacy interchangeable.
The first narrative implementation should select two or three such responses,
not build the whole social model at once.

Repeated incidents can deepen a response but must not produce infinite bonuses.
A rescue can be credited once to its actual participants. Issuing and canceling
an escort repeatedly cannot farm trust. A rumor is a claim with a source, not a
rewrite of the witnessed history. Death leaves memories in survivors and places;
it does not automatically make everyone braver or more obedient.

## 8. Occupation and political consequences

Military capture changes who controls a position. It does not sell or transfer
the residents. A hen, rooster, chick or tree keeps identity, family, memories and
attachments. Residents may remain, flee, cooperate, bargain, resist or later
affiliate with the new kingdom. Those choices require circumstances and causes.

For a later inhabited-occupation design, use explicit local arrangements:
continued access to food and home; stated guard duties; a supply contribution;
a promise of protection; or permission for an old household to leave. None grants
instant loyalty. Compliance can be fearful or pragmatic, and resistance can be
self-interested rather than universally righteous.

Garrison size and logistics establish what an occupier can enforce. An agreement
can reduce the required force but creates an obligation with observable terms.
Breaking it changes future behavior. The player may win the battle and face a
costly holding; abandoning it can preserve the army while losing the strategic
benefit. This makes consolidation part of conquest rather than an unrelated
morality minigame.

Kinship, marriage and fostering can later bind kingdoms, but they never function
as trading an anonymous hen for a diplomatic bonus. A named participant's stance,
earlier ties and subsequent life remain visible. These systems are outside the
first warfare prototype and should not be prerequisites for its victory.

## 9. Tone and presentation

Use a few well-timed observations, work animations and changed behavior. Most
events should not pause play. A short aftermath or an inspected history can
explain what mattered after the immediate command problem is over.

Proposed lines illustrate voice, not automatic text triggers:

- Tansy, when an unsupplied expedition is proposed: “The plan has reached the
  far grove. Supper has not.”
- Bracken, after holding a retreat: “I said I would be last. I had hoped that
  would sound less literal.”
- Sedge, inspecting a map after a flank: “They have fortified the entrance.
  Fortunately, the grove has several opinions about where it begins.”
- Reedwake, after a military victory leaves its homes empty: “There are more
  guards now. Fewer goodnights.”

Comedy belongs to pride, effort, ceremonial importance and conflicting views.
An elaborate declaration can be interrupted by a necessary delivery. The player
can appreciate a splendidly self-important captain without pretending a wound
or death is meaningless. Do not use distressed children as routine comic props
or manufacture constant tragedy to make the simulation seem profound.

## 10. History and endings

A scenario's closing account should explain the campaign in concrete terms:
which groves were held, how supply changed, which approaches mattered, who
returned, who died, which promises were kept and what relationships changed.
It should support the player's memory, not summarize a parallel authored plot.

Two victories with identical borders can leave different kingdoms. One has a
healthy reserve and a respected quartermaster; another has an admired commander,
an exhausted care household and a new holding whose residents remain hostile.
These differences should affect a later scenario only when campaign persistence
is explicitly implemented. A decorative ending line is not proof of that system.

The proposed long-term narrative is a history of expanding kingdoms and changing
generations. Particular campaigns create veterans, rivals, refugees, founders
and remembered places. A descendant can inherit a relationship to a place without
inheriting a fixed profession or moral destiny. The short first scenario needs
only enough continuity to make its own result matter.

## 11. Narrative acceptance and creative handoff

Before creative production, evaluate whether the conflict offers reasons to
fight, meaningful alternatives in how to fight, and a society whose individuals
remain people after defeat. Check each scenario against an actual economic and
territorial decision. A dramatic scene without a mechanical cause is not enough.

For the first eventual playable test, require one named contribution recognized
in the outcome, one injury or rescue with a saved consequence, and one lasting
response that changes a later feasible assignment. The test should not force
the same death, hero or opening order every run. All history must survive refresh.

Creative design next should establish the silhouettes of roles and communities,
how equipment works with chicken anatomy, how a grove reads as contested or
occupied, and how pride and fatigue appear at the strategy camera. It should
draw from these characters and rules rather than inventing incompatible powers
or a different kind of civilization. See the
[creative handoff](../../art-direction/chicken-civ-creative-handoff.md).
